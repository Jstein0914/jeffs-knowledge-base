# Analyzing the Smithsonian Institution's Organizing System

The [Smithsonian Institution's website](https://www.si.edu/) provides access to more than 18.7 million searchable records from its museums, archives, and libraries. Organizing a collection this large requires more than cataloging individual objects. The system must also organize digital materials, descriptive information, and the connections that allow users to discover resources across different Smithsonian collections.

---

## Resources

The Smithsonian organizes more than the physical objects held by its museums and research facilities. The website also organizes digital resources and information about the items in its collections. For example, [Akili Ron Anderson's *The Last Supper*](https://nmaahc.si.edu/object/nmaahc_2021.73.1) is a physical artwork that remained at its original location, while the Smithsonian created a digital 3D model and a record containing information about the artwork. In contrast, [Devin Allen's *Untitled*](https://nmaahc.si.edu/object/nmaahc_2016.98.2) photograph is born digital, meaning the digital file itself is the original collection resource. In both cases, metadata such as creator, date, subject, location, and history provides useful information for the user.

## Interactions

The Smithsonian gives users several ways to find and interact with its collections, whether they are exploring a general interest or looking for something specific. [Smithsonian Explore](https://www.si.edu/explore) allows users to begin with broad interests and discover resources without a specific search in mind, while the [Collections Search Center](https://collections.si.edu/search/) allows users to search for something specific, narrow the results, examine individual records, and save items for later. Users can also do more than find information. Through the [Smithsonian Transcription Center](https://transcription.si.edu/), volunteers transcribe and review collection materials, making them more accessible and easier to search. This allows users to not only explore the Smithsonian's collections but also help make those collections easier for others to discover.

## Classification System

The Smithsonian primarily uses faceted classification while also using a hierarchical style of organization. In Collections Search, users can narrow results through facets such as type, topic, date, catalog source, and availability of online media. This works well because the Smithsonian website brings together collections from many museums, archives, and libraries. Users can also browse from broad subject areas into specific museums and collections. Together, these options give casual visitors and researchers different ways to find what they are looking for, although some specialized terms may be unfamiliar to casual visitors.

## Relationships Between Resources

The Smithsonian represents relationships that allow users to move from one resource to others without beginning a new search. Shared subjects can connect resources across the collections, while links such as “See more items in” connect an individual record to its larger collection or Smithsonian institution. Archival records preserve another type of relationship by showing where a resource belongs within a collection, series, box, or folder. These connections provide more than navigation because they give resources context. A user can follow the relationship most relevant to what they are looking for, whether that means exploring a shared subject, examining a larger collection, or understanding where an archival item fits within its original structure.
