# Olympic Records and Dominance

Olympic dominance can take many forms, including career medal records, repeated championships, and exceptional performances at a single Olympics. Medal totals are one measure, but athletes in some sports have more opportunities to win medals than others. An athlete may also dominate by repeatedly winning the same event or remaining successful across several Olympics. Consecutive titles, longevity, and the length of time a record stands can therefore add important context to an Olympic achievement.

## Career Olympic Records

Career Olympic records are built across multiple Olympics, making longevity part of the achievement. American swimmer [Michael Phelps](https://www.olympics.com/en/athletes/michael-phelps-ii) competed at five Olympics and became the most decorated Olympian in history with 28 medals, including 23 golds. Before Phelps, Soviet gymnast Larisa Latynina held the career record with 18 medals, including nine golds, won between 1956 and 1964. Career records can also come from repeatedly winning the same event rather than competing in several events at each Olympics.

### Single-Games Dominance

Some athletes have produced remarkable results during a single Olympics. American swimmer Mark Spitz won seven gold medals at Munich 1972, setting a record that stood until Michael Phelps won eight at Beijing 2008. At the 1980 Winter Olympics in Lake Placid, American speed skater Eric Heiden won all five men’s speed-skating events. Unlike Phelps and Spitz, who could compete in several swimming events and relays, Heiden won every available men’s event in his sport at those Olympics.
