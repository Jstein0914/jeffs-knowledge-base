# Social and Political Milestones

Some Olympic achievements become historically significant because of the circumstances surrounding them. At [[summer-games/history-of-the-summer-games|Berlin 1936]], American track and field athlete Jesse Owens won four gold medals at an Olympics that Nazi Germany used to promote its image and racial ideology. His victories became especially significant because a Black athlete was succeeding on an international stage while the host government promoted ideas of racial superiority. Other Olympic milestones have involved athletes breaking racial or gender barriers, representing communities that had faced exclusion, or using their Olympic visibility to address social and political issues.

## Breaking Social Barriers

Some athletes have reached Olympic milestones while facing barriers that extended beyond sport. At London 1948, American high jumper Alice Coachman became the first Black woman to win an Olympic gold medal. She achieved that distinction during an era of racial segregation in the United States that restricted where Black athletes could train and compete. At Sydney 2000, Aboriginal Australian athlete Cathy Freeman lit the Olympic cauldron during the opening ceremony and later won gold in the 400 meters. Her role at those Olympics carried particular significance in Australia because of her Aboriginal identity and her association with reconciliation.

### Olympic Protest and Political Expression

Olympic athletes have also used the international attention surrounding the Games to make social or political statements. At [[olympic-venues/olympic-venue-locations|Mexico City 1968]], Americans Tommie Smith and John Carlos won gold and bronze in the 200 meters and raised gloved fists during the medal ceremony as part of a protest against racial inequality. Australian silver medalist Peter Norman supported them by wearing an Olympic Project for Human Rights badge. Smith and Carlos faced immediate consequences for the demonstration, but the image of the three athletes on the medal podium became one of the most recognized moments of protest in Olympic history.
